## Mission

The Games and Simulation Lab uses interactive environments to make algorithms, probability, agents, and real-time constraints visible. Projects should emphasize measurable behavior and educational value rather than engine breadth.

A larger flagship project in this track should be a mobile-first game that demonstrates both gameplay depth and engineering discipline: clear rules, readable telemetry, repeatable testing, and performance awareness for phones. It can also use references from psychology, philosophy, and sociology to challenge attention, decision-making, ambiguity tolerance, emotional resilience, self-control, the reading of power relations, and the player's very sense of identity. The goal is to create a mental-training and existential-and-social-reflection experience where the user must think quickly, revise assumptions, tolerate controlled frustration, adapt behavior under pressure, and confront different ways of interpreting and organizing reality.

## Expected outcomes

- Turn study into executable, tested, and demonstrable software.
- Record assumptions, decisions, limitations, and evidence reproducibly.
- Deliver integrable components without unnecessary coupling.

## Proposed flagship concept

A strong portfolio direction is a mobile-ready game such as **Atlas Run**: a 2D action/arcade experience with short sessions, procedural challenges, upgrade choices, lightweight AI opponents, and scenarios that force the player to interpret ambiguous signals, moral dilemmas, and patterns of behavior. The project should prioritize:

- one clear core loop that works well on touch screens;
- deterministic gameplay rules that are easy to test;
- controlled psychological pressure, such as uncertainty, tight timing, meaningful trade-offs, rapid context reading, and choices that reveal personal preference;
- telemetry for retention, session length, error rate, hesitation, pause patterns, strategy shifts, and difficulty balance;
- offline-friendly progression or save-state behavior;
- feedback design that encourages attention, working memory, self-correction, choice review, learning from mistakes, and reflection on behavior;
- a clean handoff between gameplay logic, UI, analytics, and behavioral experiments.

## Psychological dimension of the project

The game can use references from psychology and philosophy to provoke reflection and learning, for example:

- the classic stimulus-response tension, with variations in reinforcement and reward;
- the influence of attention, memory, and perception on player choice;
- the tension between freedom, determinism, and responsibility;
- the distinction between useful pain, constructive frustration, and emotional blockage;
- the comparison between behaviorist, cognitivist, humanistic, existentialist, psychoanalytic, and neuroscientific perspectives;
- the way narrative, symbols, and moral dilemmas shape interpretation and decision.

## Historical reference map

The project can be designed to engage major traditions across intellectual history:

- Psychology: structuralism, functionalism, behaviorism, Gestalt, psychoanalysis, cognitivism, humanism, social psychology, developmental psychology, positive psychology, and neuroscience.
- Philosophy: Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle; Stoicism, skepticism, and Epicureanism; rationalism and empiricism; Kant; pragmatism; phenomenology; existentialism; utilitarianism; virtue ethics; analytic and continental philosophy.
- Cross-cutting themes: mind and body, perception and reality, morality, intention, habit, fear, guilt, freedom, suffering, and purpose.

These mechanisms should be used transparently, with clear purpose and ethical guardrails rather than arbitrary deception or coercion.

## Manifesto for human depth

The game must not treat psychology, philosophy, and sociology as scholarly decoration, a collection of quotations, or a classroom lecture. These fields should form the grammar of gameplay: determining which information is hidden, how a reward changes behavior, how an institution distributes power, when a narrative produces identity, why a rule appears legitimate, and what happens when players realize that the system is also observing, classifying, and transforming them.

The experience should be complex, dense, and intellectually aggressive. It should provoke, contradict, unsettle certainty, frustrate expectations, present temptations, induce hypotheses, and train new forms of attention. It may confront players with guilt, desire, fear, shame, resentment, compassion, cognitive dissonance, ambivalence, and symbolic loss of control. It may ask them to obey a rule before revealing its effects, reward a strategy and later expose its collective cost, create identification with a morally ambiguous character, or turn an apparently private choice into a political event. Provocation must be substantive: shock alone is not enough; it must produce interpretation, belief revision, metacognition, and a genuine possibility of change.

The system may manipulate pace, framing, salience, expectation, and feedback—as every interactive work does—but that capacity exists to enrich the player's experience, never to diminish autonomy. “Training” here means giving players a safe space to practice perception, self-control, reasoning, coordination, and social reading, not manufacturing dependency, submission, or compulsive consumption. Intensity must coexist with care: responsive controls, clear language, fair difficulty, pauses, recovery after emotional peaks, accessibility, operational predictability, and freedom to refuse, review, or leave sensitive experiences.

## Psychological resources applied to design

Psychological mechanisms should be deliberate, measurable, and tied to an explicit design hypothesis:

- **Attention and perception:** exogenous and endogenous orienting, salience, contrast, habituation, dishabituation, vigilance, selective attention, inattentional blindness, priming, and Gestalt principles—proximity, similarity, closure, continuity, and figure-ground—can teach players to distinguish signal, noise, and framing.
- **Memory and learning:** sensory memory, working memory, chunking, spaced repetition, active recall, interference, forgetting, cognitive maps, observational learning, insight, and transfer. The game may remember prior choices meaningfully without pretending to know the person better than it does.
- **Conditioning and reinforcement:** classical and operant conditioning, positive and negative reinforcement, extinction, shaping, generalization, discrimination, reinforcement schedules, and reward-prediction error. Variable rewards should exist only when they serve an expressive or educational purpose, never as opaque machinery for compulsion, predatory monetization, or retention at any cost.
- **Cognition and decision-making:** bounded rationality, heuristics, confirmation bias, availability, anchoring, loss aversion, framing, sunk costs, temporal discounting, overconfidence, choice overload, prospect theory, exploration versus exploitation, and decisions under uncertainty. The goal is to make these processes observable and debatable, not to use them silently against the user.
- **Motivation and agency:** intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, self-determination theory—autonomy, competence, and relatedness—self-efficacy, epistemic curiosity, flow, mastery goals, implementation intentions, and informational feedback. Progression should communicate genuine growth rather than create artificial scarcity or fear of loss.
- **Emotion and regulation:** arousal, valence, suspense, surprise, catharsis, cognitive appraisal, frustration tolerance, reappraisal, gradual exposure, and recovery. Peaks of tension need counterpoints of safety, humor, beauty, silence, attachment, and rest so that intensity does not become exhaustion.
- **Identity and personality:** persona, shadow, ego ideal, self-concept, narrative identity, social identity, roles, values, defense mechanisms, and moral development. Psychological profiles must remain local hypotheses inside the game, never clinical diagnoses or definitive labels applied to a person.
- **Relationship and social influence:** reciprocity, trust, reputation, conformity, obedience, normative and informational influence, social comparison, diffusion of responsibility, the bystander effect, group polarization, cooperation, competition, and commons dilemmas. Players should perceive how social context changes what they call an individual choice.
- **Narrative and meaning-making:** archetypes, symbols, metaphors, unreliable narrators, interpretive gaps, autobiographical memory, narrative transportation, and identification should sustain multiple readings without reducing traditions to stereotypes or pseudoscientific varnish.

## Philosophical map: from the pre-Socratics to contemporary thought

The game should engage divergent traditions by putting their concepts into conflict through rules, scenarios, and consequences:

- **Pre-Socratic thought:** change and permanence in Heraclitus and Parmenides; archē, nature, number, atomism, chance, necessity, plurality, and logos among the Milesians, Pythagoreans, Empedocles, Anaxagoras, Leucippus, and Democritus.
- **Classical antiquity:** Socratic inquiry, care of the self, truth, and rhetoric; forms, appearances, and justice in Plato; causation, virtue, habit, politics, and flourishing in Aristotle; tensions among sophistry, Cynicism, Cyrenaicism, and competing accounts of the good life.
- **Hellenistic and Roman schools:** Stoicism, Epicureanism, skepticism, Cynicism, and Neoplatonism, including their answers to desire, suffering, tranquility, judgment, fate, pleasure, suspension of assent, and self-mastery.
- **Medieval and non-European philosophies:** patristics, scholasticism, nominalism, mysticism, Jewish and Islamic philosophy; Indian, Buddhist, Jain, Daoist, and Confucian thought; African and Indigenous American philosophies and Ubuntu. The project should acknowledge internal differences and avoid presenting the European canon as synonymous with universal thought.
- **Renaissance and modernity:** humanism, Machiavelli, social-contract theory, rationalism, empiricism, materialism, idealism, liberalism, and the Enlightenment; Cartesian doubt, conatus, monads, personal identity, causality, the state of nature, sovereignty, property, and the general will.
- **Modern systems and critique:** the limits of reason, autonomy, and duty in Kant; dialectic, recognition, and history in Hegel; utilitarianism; Romanticism; positivism; pragmatism; pessimism; and critiques of religion, morality, alienation, ideology, and capital in Feuerbach, Marx, Kierkegaard, Schopenhauer, Nietzsche, Peirce, James, and Dewey.
- **Twentieth- and twenty-first-century thought:** phenomenology, hermeneutics, existentialism, philosophy of language, the analytic tradition, critical theory, structuralism, post-structuralism, deconstruction, philosophy of mind, care ethics, communitarianism, postcolonialism, decolonial thought, feminisms, queer theory, critical race theory, philosophy of technology, transhumanism, new materialism, speculative realism, environmental philosophy, and AI ethics.
- **Persistent problems:** being and becoming; mind and body; appearance and reality; language and world; knowledge and skepticism; freedom and determinism; virtue, duty, and consequence; absurdity and meaning; individual and community; human and machine; life, death, memory, responsibility, and the future.

These schools should not become simplistic factions with one “correct” answer. A mechanic might invite a Stoic interpretation in one situation, a Marxist critique in another, a Foucauldian analysis of discipline in a third, and a feminist or decolonial challenge to the assumptions shared by all three. The game becomes deeper when a solution that works efficiently in one system becomes morally uncomfortable in another.

## Psychological map: schools, conflicts, and complementarities

The psychological repertoire should span structuralism and functionalism; methodological, radical, and social behaviorisms; reflexology; Gestalt psychology; genetic and cultural-historical psychology; Freudian, Kleinian, Winnicottian, and Lacanian psychoanalysis; analytical and individual psychology; attachment, object-relations, and psychosocial-development theories; cognitivism, constructivism, predictive processing, and embodied, situated, enactive, and distributed cognition; humanism, person-centered therapy, and Gestalt therapy; existential, phenomenological, social, community, cultural, moral, developmental, positive, ecological, and evolutionary psychologies; neuropsychology, affective neuroscience, and contemporary behavioral science.

The project may dramatize conflicts between the unconscious and deliberation, stimulus and meaning, individual and environment, trait and situation, cognition and body, adaptation and emancipation. It must distinguish scientific evidence, clinical model, interpretive tradition, and artistic metaphor. Clinical concepts must not diagnose players, trivialize psychological suffering, or promise treatment. If the game addresses trauma, grief, compulsion, dissociation, self-harm, abuse, or suicide, it will require content notices, specialist review, intensity-reduction options, and appropriate support resources.

## Sociological and political map

The sociological dimension should show that behavior does not emerge only “inside” a person. It is produced and constrained by norms, institutions, technologies, classes, markets, families, states, communities, languages, and historical power relations:

- **Founders and classical sociology:** order and positivism in Comte; historical materialism, class, exploitation, and alienation in Marx; social facts, solidarity, anomie, and ritual in Durkheim; social action, rationalization, bureaucracy, authority, and disenchantment in Weber; sociability and metropolitan life in Simmel.
- **Structures and systems:** functionalism, systems theory, structuralism, social conflict, dependency, world-systems, hegemony, the culture industry, the public sphere, and social reproduction.
- **Interaction and everyday life:** symbolic interactionism, social dramaturgy, ethnomethodology, social phenomenology, the social construction of reality, labeling, deviance, stigma, and self-fulfilling prophecy.
- **Power and inequality:** discipline, biopolitics, governmentality, economic/cultural/social capital, habitus, field, symbolic violence, coloniality, structural racism, patriarchy, intersectionality, masculinities, disability, and the social production of difference.
- **The contemporary world:** network society, platform capitalism, surveillance, datafication, immaterial labor, precarity, algorithmic culture, epistemic bubbles, disinformation, liquid modernity, risk society, social acceleration, the Anthropocene, and collective action.

These ideas should become playable systems: unequally distributed resources; rules that benefit different groups; reputation converted into access; bureaucracies that diffuse responsibility; algorithmic classifications that appear neutral; communities that create solidarity and exclusion; markets that reshape values; propaganda that contests memory; institutions that outlive the intentions of their members; and rebellion, cooperation, organizing, care, and resistance as actions with costs and consequences.

## Architecture of provocation

Each chapter, stage, or experiment should declare a central human question and translate it into experience. Examples include: “Do you still call it a choice when it was carefully framed?”, “Is an efficient decision still right when its cost is invisible?”, “Who do you become when the system rewards your worst strategy?”, “Is obedience less violent than action?”, “Does your identity remain the same when your memory changes?”, and “Can you care for someone without controlling them?”

Progression can alternate five movements:

1. **Welcome:** establish operational safety, legibility, connection, curiosity, and initial competence.
2. **Induction:** create an expectation, habit, interpretation, or alliance through the rules themselves.
3. **Rupture:** introduce contradiction, hidden cost, social perspective, or evidence that disrupts the previous mental model.
4. **Reflection:** offer time, silence, dialogue, comparison, journaling, replay, consequence visualization, or a shift in point of view.
5. **Reappropriation:** return agency to players, allowing them to formulate a strategy, reject the proposed frame, invent a third alternative, or transform the system.

This architecture allows the game to be incisive without becoming hostile and welcoming without becoming harmless. The interface should invite interaction; the content may challenge deeply. Players should feel that the system pays attention, responds intelligently, and respects their time—not that it monitors them to exploit weakness.

## Ethical contract and player experience

Persuasion must not become coercion. The project rejects dark patterns, disguised advertising, guilt for absence, expiring rewards designed to manufacture anxiety, deliberately degraded progression that sells relief, excessive data collection, vulnerability-based personalization, and adaptive difficulty used to prevent players from stopping. It must not infer mental health, orientation, beliefs, financial status, or emotional fragility in order to target stimuli.

The system should improve the experience through gentle onboarding, precise feedback, adaptable and explainable difficulty, contextual consent, intensity controls, accessibility options, break reminders, no punishment for time away, privacy by default, and minimized telemetry. Emotionally difficult content should be signposted without destroying narrative surprise; players should be able to pause, skip, reduce, review, and delete data. Optional debriefings can reveal which attentional, framing, and influence techniques were used, turning persuasion itself into an object of critical literacy.

The final criterion is simple: after being provoked, players should leave with more language for understanding themselves and the world, more capacity for choice, and more curiosity—never more dependent, ashamed, isolated, or manipulable. Every psychologically intense mechanic should document its hypothesis, expected benefit, risk, safeguard, success metric, and stopping condition.

## Architectural approach

- Start with a small vertical slice containing input, rule, output, and test.
- Separate domain logic from frameworks, storage, and external interfaces.
- Prefer explicit contracts and replaceable adapters.
- Add infrastructure only when a concrete use case requires it.
- Document irreversible or high-impact decisions through ADRs.

## Quality and evidence

- Unit tests for deterministic rules and transformations.
- Integration tests at external boundaries.
- Versioned data, seeds, and configuration when required.
- Technical and product metrics appropriate to the experiment.
- Behavioral metrics on focus, error, review, pause, recovery, and strategy shifts.

- No committed secrets or personal data.
- Ethical design: the player should be able to recognize, understand, and, where appropriate, control the stimuli used.
